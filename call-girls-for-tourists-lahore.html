Cultural Etiquette: Do's and Don'ts

Lahore is warm and hospitable, and locals cut visitors plenty of slack. A little effort still goes a long way. These habits keep you comfortable in bazaars, mosques, offices, and family settings.

Greet with "Salaam Alaikum." It means peace be upon you, and it opens almost any conversation. Use your right hand for greetings, food, and handing over money. Many men shake hands warmly. With women, wait and follow their lead. Some people prefer a hand on the chest instead. Both are normal.

Dress modestly outside your hotel. Covering shoulders and knees is enough for museums, markets, and restaurants. Beachwear stays by the pool. Gulberg is relaxed, while religious sites ask for more, so carry a light scarf in your day bag.

Keep affection private. Public displays of affection between couples are not part of local culture. Holding hands in public is about the limit. Save everything else for private space. Your companion follows the same rule, and it protects you both from unwanted attention.

Ask before photographing people, especially women and children. Never photograph military installations, airports, or government buildings. In bazaars, a smile and a gesture is enough to ask. If someone says no, accept it and move on.

Ramadan changes the daytime rhythm. Eating, drinking, and smoking in public during daylight hours is disrespectful, and in some places it is an offence. Restaurants serve behind screens, and evenings become lively again. Our companions for foreign guests understand these rhythms and plan around them.

Two more notes. Pakistan is a dry country, so do not expect bars or public alcohol service, and never drink in public or drink and drive. Friday afternoons quiet down for prayers. Plan an indoor activity between noon and two.

Tipping is appreciated but not enforced. Ten per cent at restaurants is generous when no service charge appears, and a hundred or two hundred rupees for a porter is standard. On the street, use the phrases in the next section and you will get fair prices every time.

Language Basics for Bookings

English works across Lahore's hotels, restaurants, and shopping streets. Our booking team replies in English, and every tourist-facing companion speaks it fluently. You do not need Urdu to book. A few local words still smooth the ride, especially with rickshaw drivers and market sellers.

Greetings and manners. "Salaam" is hello. "Shukriya" is thank you. "Meherbani" works as a polite please. "Haan" means yes and "nahin" means no. Learning these four gets you through most exchanges with a smile.

Money phrases. "Kitna hua?" asks how much. "Thora kam" asks for a little less. Numbers matter most: ek is one, do is two, teen is three, char is four, paanch is five, and das is ten. Sau means a hundred and hazaar means a thousand. Fifteen thousand rupees comes out as "pandra hazaar," which is worth knowing before you agree a rate.

Getting around. "Yahan kaisay jaen?" means how do I get there. "Kitna waqt lagay ga?" asks how long it will take. "Rok do" tells a driver to stop. Carry your hotel's name card in Urdu so a driver can read it when signal drops cut off your map.

Roman Urdu is loose. The same phrase appears three ways in writing, so do not worry about perfect spelling. Send a voice note on WhatsApp instead. We will understand you and reply in clear English with the rate, the timing, and the address spelled out.

If you would rather practise, try one simple sentence at breakfast and let your companion correct you. Guests tell us those small lessons become the favourite part of the trip.
